How the organism pages work

Data, model, how much to trust each map, limitations and credits for the organism maps. Educational use only.

Not a forecast. Every future map here is projected climate suitability: where the climate may suit an organism, according to a statistical model. It is not a prediction of where the organism will be found. The pages are for learning and curiosity, not for planting, farming, conservation or land decisions.

What the maps show

For each organism the page shows the places where the model finds the climate suitable now (1991–2020), in the future (2041–2060 or 2081–2100, under the moderate SSP2-4.5 or the high-emissions SSP5-8.5 scenario), and the change: land that is suitable now but not later (lost, hatched orange), suitable in both (kept, grey) or suitable only later (gained, dotted blue). Colour is never the only cue: lost is hatched and gained is dotted. Suitability is drawn in three classes, not as a smooth probability.

The summary gives the area with suitable climate, its change, and how far the centre of that area moves. The centre shift is the movement of an area-weighted average position; it can move even when no single place does.

Data

  • Where the organism has been recorded: GBIF occurrence records with a CC0 or CC-BY licence, cleaned and thinned to one record per 0.125° cell (about 14 km) for counting; each organism cites the download DOI (table below). Records with other licences are not used. Organisms with fewer than 100 such cells are not modelled.
  • Native range: records are restricted to where the organism is native, using Kew’s World Checklist of Vascular Plants for plants and curated range polygons for animals. For crops this restriction does not apply.
  • Present climate: TerraClimate, 1991–2020, on a grid of 1/24° (about 4.6 km). Ten candidate measures: annual mean temperature, temperature range and seasonality, warmest-month and coldest-month temperature, annual precipitation, precipitation seasonality, driest-quarter precipitation, growing degree-days, climatic water deficit and actual evapotranspiration.
  • Future climate: the same present-day climate plus the change in each of 14 climate models from NASA’s NEX-GDDP-CMIP6 for SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5.
  • Checks against independent data: the North American Breeding Bird Survey (for birds), and, for trees, comparison with the US Forest Service’s published tree projections (RDS-2024-0020).

The model

For each organism, a gradient-boosted tree model (LightGBM) learns how the recorded places differ in climate from a sample of background places. The background is drawn where observers of the same group of organisms (for example, all birds) have recorded things, which corrects part of the bias towards well-visited places. At most five climate measures are kept, chosen in an order based on the organism’s ecology, and a measure is dropped when it is strongly correlated (above 0.7) with one already kept. A second, simpler model (a smooth additive model) is run as a check; when the two disagree strongly about the area change the organism is marked low confidence.

“Suitable” means the model score is above a threshold set so that 95% of held-out records fall inside. The model is projected under each of 14 climate models and the median is shown; the share of models that agree can be shaded on the map (dotted where fewer than 3 in 4 agree).

Migratory birds use the breeding-season fit: only records from the breeding months (May to July in the north) and a breeding-season native range. Their pages say so. Resident birds, and animals such as the monarch, use all records.

Crops are shown where the climate is like where the crop is recorded, in the exact wording on each page. Unless the records are verified as cultivated (for the wine grape they are not), this is not a statement about where the crop can be grown.

Dispersal: three bounds

An organism cannot occupy all newly suitable land. Each future map can be read with three bounds: unlimited (any suitable area counts), limited (new area counts only within a reach of today’s range) and none (only the part of today’s range that stays suitable). The limited reach comes from a rule of thumb by group: about 15 km per decade for trees, 10 for crops and reptiles, 40 for insects and ticks, 50 for mammals and 100 for birds. These distances are uncalibrated and probably uncertain by a factor of about 10. The truth for a given organism usually lies between the limited and unlimited bounds, or below none if the range retreats faster than it can shift.

How much to trust a map: two checks

Every organism shows two plain checks, because “is today’s map right?” and “can the future map be trusted?” are different questions.

Today’s map: checked
The model’s map of today’s suitable climate was checked against an independent survey it had never seen (so far, the Breeding Bird Survey for birds and US Forest Inventory plots for trees: discrimination AUC at least 0.7 and best-threshold TSS at least 0.4). Internally this is Tier A.
Today’s map: fits the records
Checked by spatial-block cross-validation: the model is trained on some regions and tested on others. This is a fair test of skill in new places, but no independent survey exists for most organisms, so it is not checked against one. Internally this is Tier B. It is the normal case, not a warning.
Today’s map: looser fit
As above, but the fit is weaker than we would like (for example, discrimination below 0.7 or much of the suitable area far from any record). The reason is on the organism’s page.
Not shown
Organisms below the skill or range-check gates are not published (Tier C).

The page also states the cross-validation kind: “presence against background” compares records with a sample of background places, which is a weaker test than “presence and absence”.

The second check, Future map, is explained next. The future map always shows where climate may suit the organism, never a forecast of where it will live.

Range shifts: pass, fail, untested

The Future map check records whether we could show that models like this one can predict real range changes. We fitted the model to records from an early period, asked it where the organism should be found in a later period, and compared that with surveys of what actually happened.

  • Fail (all birds tested): the model locates today’s ranges well, but for the real movements between the two periods the direction of change was no better than chance. In plain words: the model describes where the climate suits a bird today, but we could not show it knows where the bird will go. Treat the future map as a map of climate, and the Today’s map check as a statement about today only.
  • Untested (trees, insects and others): there was not enough survey data on real change to run the test (for example, only 5 trees against a minimum of 20). Adult trees also lag the climate, so absence of change is expected even when the climate has moved. This is not a pass.
  • Pass: the model reproduced observed change in the test. No organism has passed yet.

Withheld numbers

When more than 15% of the area projected to be suitable lies in a climate that nothing in the training data resembles (a “novel climate”, found with the MESS rule), the model would be extrapolating beyond what it has seen. The page then shows the numbers as withheld, with the reason and the share of novel climate. The map is still drawn, with those places marked by diagonal lines, so you can see where the problem lies.

Limitations

  • Dispersal. See above: the three bounds are rules of thumb, not predictions. Barriers, habitat loss and human movement are not modelled.
  • Non-climatic limits. Soils, land use, fire, water bodies, competitors, predators, pathogens, pollinators and host species are not in the model. A place can be climatically suitable and have none of what the organism needs. The native-range check catches only some of this.
  • Migratory handling. Breeding-season fits describe the breeding range only. Winter ranges, stopover places and migration routes are not modelled. For the barn swallow the native mask has no South America, so its wintering range there is absent. Month information is missing for some records and they are left out of breeding-season fits. The background still reflects year-round observer effort.
  • Precipitation sensitivity. The change in rainfall in the future climate data is less well validated than the change in temperature. In a test on two trees, setting the rainfall change to zero changed the 2081–2100 area change by up to 17 percentage points, and the centre shift by only 10 to 50 km. Read area changes for organisms that depend on rainfall or water deficit with that in mind; the direction and size of the centre shift are more robust.
  • Agreement with other models. For trees, the size and direction of the centre shift are similar to the US Forest Service’s projections, but not identical. For quaking aspen our shift is about half the size and the area change is smaller (about +5% against +35 to +59%). These are two models agreeing or disagreeing, not tests against what happened.
  • Resolution. Cells are about 4.6 km. Mountains, coasts and small ranges are coarsely represented, and narrow-range organisms are less reliable.
  • Records. Only CC0 and CC-BY records are used, which removes a large share of observations of some organisms (for example about half for the monarch and tick in earlier tests). Records reflect where people look.
  • Thresholds. Several limits (novelty 15%, range-check and agreement limits, the dispersal distances) are first values that have not been calibrated.
